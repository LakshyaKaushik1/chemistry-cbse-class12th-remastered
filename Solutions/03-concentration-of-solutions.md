# 1.2 Expressing Concentration of Solutions

## 1. What is Concentration?

Concentration tells us how much solute is present in a given amount of solution.

There are two ways to describe concentration:

1. Qualitative: Using words like dilute and concentrated.
2. Quantitative: Using mathematical values and formulas.

- Dilute solution: Contains a relatively small amount of solute.
- Concentrated solution: Contains a relatively large amount of solute.

**Why do we need quantitative methods?**

Words like dilute and concentrated are not precise. Their meaning can vary from person to person. Therefore, we use mathematical methods to express the exact concentration.

There are 7 important methods of expressing concentration.

---

## 2. Different Methods of Expressing Concentration

### (i) Mass Percentage (w/w)

**Meaning:** It tells us how many grams of a component are present in 100 g of solution.

**Formula:**

$$\text{Mass Percentage of a component} = \frac{\text{Mass of Component}}{\text{Total Mass of Solution}} \times 100$$

**Example:**

A solution contains 10% glucose by mass.

This means:
- Glucose = 10 g
- Water = 90 g
- Total solution = 100 g

Remember: 

Total mass of solution = Mass of solute + Mass of solvent.

**Exam Tip:**
If a question says 10% glucose by mass, it means 10 g glucose in 100 g TOTAL solution, not 100 g water. Water is 90g.

---

### (ii) Volume Percentage (V/V)

**Meaning:** It tells us how many mL of a component are present in 100 mL of solution.

**Formula:**

### $$\text{Volume Percentage of a component} = \frac{\text{Volume of component}}{\text{Total Volume of Solution}} \times 100$$

**Example:**

A 10% ethanol solution means:
- Ethanol = 10 mL
- Total solution = 100 mL

Water is added until the TOTAL volume becomes 100 mL. So, here water will be 90mL. If there was another question saying 30mL of component A, then the water would be 70mL.

**Application:** Commonly used for solutions containing liquids.

Example:
A 35% (V/V) ethylene glycol solution is used as antifreeze in car engines.

It lowers the freezing point of water to:
- 255.4 K
- -17.6°C

**Exam Tip:**
Do not confuse the volume of solvent with the total volume of solution.

---

### (iii) Mass by Volume Percentage (w/V)

**Meaning:** It tells us how many grams of solute are present in 100 mL of solution.

**Formula:**

$$\text{Volume Percentage} = \frac{\text{Mass of solute in grams}}{\text{Volume of solution in mL}} \times 100$$

**Example:**

A 5% (w/V) glucose solution means:

5 g of glucose is present in 100 mL of solution.

**Application:**
Commonly used in:
- Medicine
- Pharmacy

**Important Difference:**

- w/w = grams per 100 g of solution.
- V/V = mL per 100 mL of solution.
- w/V = grams per 100 mL of solution.

---

### (iv) Parts Per Million (ppm)

**Meaning:** Used when the amount of solute is extremely small.

**Formula:**

$$\text{Parts per million (ppm)} = \frac{\text{No of parts of component}}{\text{Total number of parts of solution}} \times10^6$$

It can be expressed as:
- Mass by mass
- Volume by volume
- Mass by volume

**Example:**

Sea water contains approximately 5.8 ppm of dissolved oxygen.

This means 5.8 parts of oxygen are present in every 10⁶ parts of sea water by mass.

**Applications:**
- Measuring pollutants in water.
- Measuring pollutants in the atmosphere.
- Measuring very small concentrations of substances.

**Exam Tip:**

1 ppm = 1 (part) in 10⁶ (parts).

For dilute aqueous solutions, 1 ppm is approximately equal to 1 mg/L when the density is close to 1 kg/L.

---

### (v) Mole Fraction (x)

**Meaning:** Mole fraction tells us what fraction of the total moles belongs to a particular component.

**Formula:**

Mole fraction of a component = (Moles of that component / Total moles of all components)

For a solution containing two components A and B:

xA = nA / (nA + nB)

xB = nB / (nA + nB)

Where:
- nA = Moles of A
- nB = Moles of B

**Very Important Property:**

The sum of mole fractions of all components is always equal to 1.

xA + xB = 1

For multiple components:

x1 + x2 + x3 + ... + xi = 1

Therefore:

Mole fraction is a unitless quantity (it has no unit).

**Application:**
Mole fraction is useful in:
- Vapour pressure calculations.
- Calculations involving gas mixtures.

#### Solved Example 1: Mole Fraction

Calculate the mole fraction of ethylene glycol (C₂H₆O₂) in a solution containing 20% ethylene glycol by mass.

**Step 1: Assume 100 g of solution.**

Since the solution contains 20% ethylene glycol:

- Ethylene glycol = 20 g
- Water = 80 g

**Step 2: Calculate molar masses.**

Molar mass of ethylene glycol:

= (2 × 12) + (6 × 1) + (2 × 16)

= 62 g/mol

Molar mass of water:

= 18 g/mol

**Step 3: Calculate moles.**

Moles = Mass / Molar mass

Moles of ethylene glycol:

= 20 / 62

= 0.322 mol

Moles of water:

= 80 / 18

= 4.444 mol

**Step 4: Calculate mole fraction.**

Mole fraction of ethylene glycol:

x(glycol) = 0.322 / (0.322 + 4.444)

= 0.068

Mole fraction of water:

x(water) = 4.444 / (0.322 + 4.444)

= 0.932

**Final Answer:**

- Mole fraction of ethylene glycol = 0.068
- Mole fraction of water = 0.932

Check:

0.068 + 0.932 = 1 ✓

**Shortcut:** Once you calculate the mole fraction of one component, you can calculate the other by subtracting it from 1.

xB = 1 - xA

### (vi) Molarity (M)

**Meaning:** Molarity tells us the number of moles of solute dissolved in 1 litre of TOTAL solution.

**Formula:**

Molarity (M) = Moles of solute / Volume of solution in litres

M = n / V

Where:
- n = Moles of solute
- V = Volume of solution in litres.

**Example:**

A 0.25 M NaOH solution means:

0.25 moles of NaOH are dissolved in enough solvent to make 1 litre of solution.

**Important Units:**

- mol/L
- mol/dm³
- M

All three represent molarity.

#### Solved Example 2: Molarity

Calculate the molarity of a solution containing 5 g of NaOH in 450 mL of solution.

**Step 1: Calculate molar mass of NaOH.**

NaOH = 23 + 16 + 1 = 40 g/mol

**Step 2: Calculate moles.**

Moles = Mass / Molar mass

= 5 / 40

= 0.125 mol

**Step 3: Convert volume into litres.**

450 mL = 450 / 1000 L

= 0.450 L

**Step 4: Calculate molarity.**

M = Moles / Volume in litres

= 0.125 / 0.450

= 0.278 M

**Final Answer: 0.278 M**

**Important Exam Shortcut:**

When mass and volume are given directly:

M = (Mass of solute × 1000) / (Molar mass × Volume in mL)

Remember: The volume must be the volume of the TOTAL solution, not the volume of solvent.

---

### (vii) Molality (m)

**Meaning:** Molality tells us the number of moles of solute dissolved in 1 kg of SOLVENT.

**Formula:**

Molality (m) = Moles of solute / Mass of solvent in kg

m = n / W

Where:
- n = Moles of solute.
- W = Mass of solvent in kg.

**Example:**

A 1 molal KCl solution means:

1 mole (74.5 g) of KCl is dissolved in 1 kg of water.

**Important Units:**
- mol/kg
- mol kg⁻¹
- m

All three represent molality.

#### Solved Example 3: Molality

Calculate the molality of 2.5 g of ethanoic acid (CH₃COOH) dissolved in 75 g of benzene.

**Step 1: Calculate molar mass of ethanoic acid.**

CH₃COOH = C₂H₄O₂

= (2 × 12) + (4 × 1) + (2 × 16)

= 60 g/mol

**Step 2: Calculate moles of ethanoic acid.**

Moles = Mass / Molar mass

= 2.5 / 60

= 0.0417 mol

**Step 3: Convert mass of solvent into kg.**

Mass of benzene = 75 g

= 75 / 1000

= 0.075 kg

**Step 4: Calculate molality.**

m = Moles of solute / Mass of solvent in kg

= 0.0417 / 0.075

= 0.556 mol kg⁻¹

**Final Answer: 0.556 m**

## 3. Very Important: Difference Between Molarity and Molality

This is one of the most important concepts in this section.

| Molarity (M) | Molality (m) |
|---|---|
| Moles of solute per litre of solution | Moles of solute per kg of solvent |
| Uses volume | Uses mass |
| Volume of solution is used | Mass of solvent is used |
| Depends on temperature | Independent of temperature |

### Why does molarity depend on temperature?

Because volume changes with temperature.

When temperature increases:
- Volume generally increases.
- Molarity decreases (if the number of moles remains constant).

When temperature decreases:
- Volume generally decreases.
- Molarity increases.

### Why is molality independent of temperature?

Because mass does not change with temperature.

Therefore, molality remains constant when temperature changes.

---

## 4. Temperature Dependence: Important Exam Concept

Different concentration units behave differently with temperature.

| Concentration unit | Depends on temperature? |
|---|---|
| Mass percentage (w/w) | No |
| Volume percentage (V/V) | Yes |
| Mass by volume percentage (w/V) | Yes |
| Parts per million (ppm) | No, for a fixed mass-based definition |
| Mole fraction | No |
| Molarity (M) | Yes |
| Molality (m) | No |

**NCERT's important statement:**

Mass %, ppm, mole fraction and molality are independent of temperature, whereas molarity depends on temperature.

**Reason:** Volume changes with temperature, but mass does not.

Note: The table distinguishes volume-based concentration units using their usual fixed-volume definitions. The NCERT specifically highlights the temperature independence of mass %, ppm, mole fraction and molality.

# 6. Important Exam Points and Tricky Facts

1. Mole fraction is always unitless.
2. The sum of mole fractions of all components is always 1.
3. Molarity depends on temperature because volume changes with temperature.
4. Molality does not depend on temperature because mass remains constant.
5. Molarity uses the volume of solution, NOT solvent.
6. Molality uses the mass of solvent, NOT solution.
7. ppm is used for extremely small concentrations (trace quantities).
8. Mass percentage is commonly used in industrial chemical applications.
9. Volume percentage is commonly used for liquid-liquid solutions.
10. Mass by volume percentage is commonly used in medicine and pharmacy.

# 8. Things You Must Remember

- Molarity = Moles / Volume of solution (L)
- Molality = Moles / Mass of solvent (kg)
- Mole fraction = Moles of component / Total moles
- Sum of mole fractions = 1
- 1 L = 1000 mL
- 1 kg = 1000 g
- Molarity changes with temperature.
- Molality does not change with temperature.

**Most important distinction:**

Molarity → Volume of SOLUTION

Molality → Mass of SOLVENT
